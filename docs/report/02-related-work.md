# 2 Related Work (State-of-The-Art)

FitNova sits across three research areas, and each of its modules builds on a different body of prior work. This chapter reviews that work. It starts with the background of the three areas and the main techniques behind them, then surveys the specific systems and studies that are closest to FitNova, and ends with an analysis of what the prior work does well, where it falls short, and how those gaps shaped the design of this project.

## 2.1 Background

The workout planner belongs to the field of recommender systems. Classical recommender systems fall into two main styles. Content-based filtering recommends items whose features match what a user already likes or what the user's profile asks for, using a similarity between item attributes and the user description [7]. Collaborative filtering instead learns from the patterns of many users at once, and its most successful classical form is matrix factorization, which places users and items as vectors in a shared latent space and predicts a preference from their dot product [8]. Matrix factorization is linear, so it can miss more complicated relations between a user and an item. Neural collaborative filtering answers this by replacing the dot product with a small neural network that can learn a non-linear interaction between the user and the item, and this is the model that FitNova's planner is based on [2].

The form correction module belongs to computer vision, and to the part of it that works with the human body in video. Deep convolutional neural networks changed image recognition when a deep network trained on a large labelled image set clearly beat the older hand-designed features [9], and residual networks such as ResNet then made very deep models practical to train by letting each layer learn a small change on top of its input [10]. Going from a single image to video adds a time dimension. One strong and widely used way to handle that dimension is the R(2+1)D network, which splits a three-dimensional convolution into a spatial part and a separate temporal part, and which FitNova uses for its video models [11]. A different line of work does not look at the raw pixels at all, but first estimates the positions of the body joints. On-device pose estimators such as BlazePose made this fast enough to run in real time on a phone [12], and many fitness applications are built directly on a pose estimator of this kind. The narrower task of judging how well an action is performed, rather than which action it is, is called action quality assessment [13], and detecting a specific form error is a fine-grained case of that task.

The nutrition module sits in nutrition informatics. The energy a person needs in a day is normally estimated in two steps, first a resting metabolic rate from body measurements, then an activity factor multiplied on top of it, and the Mifflin-St Jeor equation is one of the most widely used and most accurate predictors of that resting rate [14]. On top of these fixed calculations, recent work has put machine learning and generative models into nutrition, for example to build weekly meal plans that respect calorie and nutrient limits, sometimes adding a language model so the output reads naturally [15]. A related line of research treats cooking as a language task and learns to generate the recipe text itself, and this is the task that FitNova reproduces in its recipe-text component [4]. Reviews of artificial intelligence in nutrition warn that systems like these should stay as decision support and must not replace clinical advice [5].

## 2.2 Literature Survey

Several recent systems recommend workouts from a user profile. Gedi et al. built a personalized gym recommendation system on a public dataset of 14,589 records with 18 columns of user data, such as age, body metrics, and stated goals, and trained eight machine learning models for both classification and regression on it [1]. They report that a decision tree reached very high scores, 99.95% on the test set for the regression target and 92.22% for the classification target, inside a pipeline of user input, data processing, analysis, a recommendation engine, and a feedback loop that updates with performance. Numbers that high usually mean the model has fit that one dataset very closely, and the work stays at the level of choosing a workout, with nothing said about how the exercises are performed or about nutrition. Sareen et al. went a step wider and joined fitness and diet in a single platform [16]. Their system gathers user inputs through a web form, passes them through data acquisition, preprocessing, model selection, and recommendation, and then shows several routine and meal options that adapt to the user's preferences and feedback through a React interface. These two systems are representative of a large family of profile-only recommenders. They show that a structured profile can drive a useful plan, but they treat the plan as the end of the job and do not check execution or verify the nutrition they suggest.

A second group of systems uses richer behavioural data. Ni et al. proposed FitRec, which models a user's heart rate and activity logs from wearable devices with a long short-term memory network, so that the recommendation reflects how a specific person's body responds during exercise and not only a static profile [17]. FitRec is more powerful than the profile-only systems, but it needs continuous sensor histories that an app without wearable integration does not have. Underneath these applications sit the standard recommender techniques. Matrix factorization represents users and items in a shared latent space and reads a preference from their product [8], neural collaborative filtering replaces that fixed product with a small network that can learn a non-linear interaction [2], and sequential models such as the self-attentive recommender SASRec read a user's ordered history of past choices to predict the next one [18]. The sequential models are strong when a real history of user behaviour exists, which is exactly what a new fitness platform does not have at the start, so FitNova works from the user profile and a collaborative-filtering model rather than from a sequence of past behaviour.

Most form-feedback systems in the literature are built on pose estimation. Tiwari et al. detect mistakes in squats, curls, planks, and lunges by reading MediaPipe pose landmarks and applying thresholds on joint angles and distances, and they report errors in plain language, such as an incorrect squat stance or a wrong knee position [19]. Ohri et al. build a similar personal-trainer system on BlazePose and OpenCV, drawing a skeleton from a webcam, computing joint angles, counting repetitions, and giving corrective feedback through a web interface and a chatbot for home workouts [20]. Jagadale et al. again use BlazePose to recognise several exercises and analyse posture, and they are honest about the weak points of the approach, that it is sensitive to camera position and to lighting [21]. These are practical, low-cost systems, and they work in tidy conditions, but they all inherit the reliability of the pose estimate sitting underneath them, and they reduce form to a few hand-written angle rules that a coach would treat as only a small part of good technique.

Academic work pushed the pose-based idea much further. Ogata et al. classified squat form from pose using temporal distance matrices, and ran into a clear limit when keypoints in the spinal region went missing [22]. Fieraru et al. presented AIFit, which fits a detailed 3D human model to fitness videos, segments the repetitions, and turns the joint trajectories into interpretable, human-readable feedback about what the trainee did wrong, with the angular signature of the movement at its centre [23]. AIFit is one of the strongest results in this direction and shows how much can be read from an accurate 3D reconstruction, but that accuracy is also its condition, it was built and measured with studio motion capture, and a clean 3D reconstruction is realistic in a capture lab and far less realistic in an ordinary gym. Across the whole pose-based line there is one shared weakness. Every method rests on a skeleton, and the skeleton becomes unreliable in real gyms because of camera angle, occlusion, clothing, and lighting, so a rule or a model built on top of it carries those errors forward.

A method is only as good as the data it learns from, and the datasets used for form assessment differ most in the kind of labels they carry. Fit3D, released with the AIFit work, is a large motion-capture dataset, with over three million frames of instructors and trainees performing dozens of exercises, each frame carrying an accurate 3D body configuration from a controlled capture studio [23]. The 3D ground truth is excellent, but the dataset has no labels for specific form errors, and a capture studio is a long way from a real gym, so any system that wants to detect mistakes from it has to invent the mistakes itself, usually by perturbing a clean repetition and calling the perturbation an error. A perturbation is not a real mistake, and a model trained on perturbed motion capture tends not to carry over to a real person filmed on a phone. The QEVD dataset, from Qualcomm, sits at the other end [24]. It is large and real, more than 460 hours of video from over 1,900 participants across 148 exercises, and it even contains people making common mistakes on purpose, but its labels are natural-language coaching messages, about two short pieces of spoken feedback per clip, and not a clean yes or no for each named error. Turning free coaching text into the structured per-error labels that a classifier needs is noisy, and the wording tends to follow the exercise type more than the fine detail of the form, so a model trained on labels pulled from the text can end up guessing the exercise instead of judging the movement. Fitness-AQA takes the harder and more useful path [3]. Its videos are real and recorded in the wild, and its labels are the per-error annotations made by expert trainers, a clean marker for each specific mistake. That match between real video and real per-error labels is why FitNova learns from Fitness-AQA, and why Fit3D and QEVD, useful as they are for 3D reconstruction and for language-based coaching, were set aside for the narrower task of detecting named form errors.

The task of judging how well a movement is performed, rather than which movement it is, is studied under the name action quality assessment [13], and detecting a named form error is a fine-grained case of it. The Fitness-AQA work of Parmar et al. attacks this case directly and is the basis of FitNova's form module [3]. Rather than estimating a skeleton, it learns representations straight from the raw pixels of the gym footage, with two self-supervised strategies that need no manual labels: Motion Disentangling, which learns from the structure of the barbell's motion across a repetition, for the video errors, and Cross-View Cross-Subject Pose Contrastive learning, which learns from matched poses seen across different camera views and different people, for the single-image depth error. Both are contrastive methods, the same family of idea that SimCLR made popular for images, where a network learns useful features by pulling together two views of the same example and pushing apart different examples with no labels at all [25]. After this label-free pretraining, only a small expert-labelled set is needed to train the final error detector. The strength of the route is that it never has to trust a skeleton, so it survives the real-gym conditions that break the pose-based systems.

Two later works build on the same dataset and are worth placing next to it. Gallardo et al. proposed GYMetricPose, which goes back to a pose representation but in a richer form, estimating 2D keypoints, lifting them to 3D, and feeding a graph that carries angular information through edge-gated convolutions before a binary error classifier [26]. Dibenedetto et al. instead fine-tuned a large multimodal model, LLaVA-Video, on the same dataset, reformatting the labels into a conversation with time stamps so that one model can name the error and locate it in time across different exercises [27]. The multimodal model has a real strength, it generalises across exercises with a single network and no per-exercise retraining, but it comes with limits that matter for a fair comparison. It slightly underperforms the specialised baselines, it is very weak on the rare errors such as the knees caving inward, it folds the validation split into its training data, and it is heavy to run, limited to 32 frames per clip even on GPUs with 64 GB of memory. For these reasons FitNova builds on the original Fitness-AQA method, and treats both later works as context rather than as a like-for-like comparison.

On the nutrition side the literature splits between fixed calculation and learned generation. The fixed part is well established. Daily energy is estimated from a resting metabolic rate and an activity factor, and the Mifflin-St Jeor equation remains one of the most used and most accurate predictors of that resting rate [14], which is why it is a sound backbone for any system that has to keep its calorie numbers correct. On top of this, recent systems add learned components. Papastratis et al. combine a deep generative model with a language model to produce weekly meal plans that respect calorie and nutrient limits [15], and Kalpakoglou et al. report an AI nutrition recommendation system that builds a personalized Mediterranean diet and put it through a technical validation [28]. These show that meal planning under constraints can be automated, but they also show how much care the constraint handling needs, because a generated plan that drifts off its targets is worse than no plan.

A separate and more language-focused line treats cooking itself as a generation problem. Majumder et al. learn to write personalized recipe text from a user's history on the Food.com data, conditioning the generated cooking steps on what the user has cooked before [4]. The wider research on recipes is supported by large public corpora, such as Recipe1M+, which gathered more than a million recipes and millions of food images for learning across text and vision, and which shows the scale at which recipe text is now studied [29]. Reviews of artificial intelligence in nutrition strike a careful note across all of this work, warning that systems like these should stay as decision support and must not replace professional or clinical advice [5]. FitNova is built directly around these two lessons. It makes the calorie, the macronutrient, and the recipe choices with fixed rules so the numbers can not drift, and it uses a language model only to describe the plan and never to choose food or change a number, which removes the failure modes the reviews warn about.

**Table 1. Datasets for form assessment, and their fit for detecting named form errors.**

| Dataset | Data and environment | Labels | Scale | Fit for fine-grained form-error detection |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fit3D [23] | Motion capture in a controlled studio; instructors and trainees | Accurate 3D body pose per frame; no labels for specific form errors | Over 3 million frames, 37+ exercises | Low: errors must be invented by perturbing clean repetitions, and a model learned that way does not transfer to real phone video |
| QEVD [24] | Real crowd-sourced video, including people making deliberate mistakes | Natural-language coaching messages, about two per clip; no clean per-error label | 460+ hours, 1,900+ participants, 148 exercises | Low to medium: structured labels must be pulled from free text, which is noisy and tracks the exercise more than the form |

## 2.3 Analysis of the Related Work

Looking across the three areas together, a clear pattern shows up. In each one, prior work has solved a part of the problem well but leaves a gap that matters for a platform that wants to be trusted. The recommenders personalize a plan but stop there. The form systems give feedback but lean on a fragile skeleton, or on data whose labels do not match real form. The nutrition systems generate plans but do not guarantee that the numbers are right. FitNova is designed around closing those specific gaps, and around one rule that runs through every choice: build on a published dataset and method, and measure with the same numbers the original work used, so each claim can be checked.

For workout recommendation, the profile-only systems of Gedi et al. and Sareen et al. show that a structured profile is enough to produce a reasonable plan, and the very high accuracy that Gedi et al. report is more a sign of close fitting to one dataset than of a hard problem solved [1], [16]. The richer systems point the other way. FitRec shows that real behavioural signals from wearables produce a more personal recommendation, but it needs continuous sensor data that a phone app without device integration does not collect [17]. Sequential recommenders such as SASRec are powerful, yet they assume a real history of user choices to read from [18], and a new platform has none. This is the honest position FitNova starts from. It has user profiles and a program catalogue but no real interaction history, so it builds the plan with a content-based filter and a neural collaborative filtering model, the method of He et al. [2], and it is open in this report that the interactions used to train that model are synthetic. The recommender therefore reproduces the NeuMF method faithfully and is measured with the same hit-rate and ranking metrics as the original work, and it is not presented as proof of real-world recommendation quality, which would need real user behaviour to claim.

For form assessment the analysis is sharper, because the prior work splits cleanly on one question, whether to trust a skeleton. The consumer systems of Tiwari, Ohri, and Jagadale, and the stronger academic system AIFit, all read the body as a set of joints and then reason about angles [19], [20], [21], [23]. This works in controlled conditions and breaks in real gyms, where the camera angle, occlusion, clothing, and lighting all damage the pose estimate, and a rule built on a damaged skeleton is damaged as well. The datasets behind this work tell the same story in a different way, summarised in Table 1. Fit3D gives perfect studio pose but no real form-error labels, so any error has to be invented and does not transfer [23]. QEVD gives real video but labels it with free coaching text that follows the exercise more than the fine detail of the form [24]. Fitness-AQA is the one dataset that pairs real in-the-wild video with clean per-error labels from expert trainers [3], which is exactly what supervised error detection needs. This is why FitNova learns directly from the pixels on Fitness-AQA, with the self-supervised pretraining of Parmar et al., instead of estimating a pose. The two later works on the same dataset confirm the choice rather than overturn it. GYMetricPose returns to a pose graph and is reported only second-hand here, with an unverified split protocol [26], and the multimodal model of Dibenedetto et al. is a single network that spans exercises but underperforms the specialised method, is weak on the rare errors, folds the validation split into training, and is far heavier to run [27]. FitNova reproduces the specialised method and reports identical per-error F1, which is the comparison that can actually be defended.

For nutrition, the generative systems of Papastratis et al. and Kalpakoglou et al. prove that meal planning under calorie and nutrient limits can be automated [15], [28], and the recipe work of Majumder et al. proves that readable cooking text can be generated from a user's history [4]. The shared risk in this line is that a learned model can drift off its targets or write something that is not real, and the reviews of AI in nutrition are explicit that such tools must stay as decision support and not replace professional advice [5]. FitNova answers this by separating the two jobs. The calorie target, the macronutrient split, and the choice of recipes are produced by fixed rules, so they are correct by construction, and the language model is only allowed to describe the plan, never to choose food or move a number. The recipe-text generator is kept as a clean reproduction of Majumder et al. on the same task and the same split, so its text quality can be compared metric for metric, while the report is clear that it does not reproduce the original paper's personalization, which it did not set out to do.

Table 2 places the most relevant systems side by side with their approach and the gap that FitNova has to cover. Read together, the prior work does not point to a single missing model, it points to a missing discipline. Each area already has methods that work in the conditions they were tested in, and the value of bringing them into one platform comes from holding every part to the same standard: a real dataset, a published method, the original metrics, and an honest statement of where the result stops being reliable. That standard is what the rest of this report follows.

**Table 2. The most relevant prior systems, their approach, and the gap FitNova must cover.**

| System or work | Area | Approach | Main limitation for FitNova's goal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gedi et al. [1] | Workout recommendation | Eight ML models on a profile dataset; a decision tree is best | Only picks a workout; near-perfect scores suggest dataset overfit; no form or nutrition |
| Sareen et al. [16] | Workout and diet | Profile to routines and meal options through a web and React app | Stops at suggestions; no execution check and no verified nutrition |
| Ni et al., FitRec [17] | Fitness recommendation | LSTM over wearable heart-rate and activity logs | Needs continuous sensor history a new app does not have |
| Tiwari, Ohri, Jagadale [19], [20], [21] | Form feedback | MediaPipe or BlazePose plus joint-angle thresholds | Skeleton unreliable in real gyms; form reduced to a few rules |
| Fieraru et al., AIFit [23] | Form feedback | 3D human model with interpretable angular feedback | Needs accurate studio 3D reconstruction, not realistic in a gym |
| Parmar et al., Fitness-AQA [3] | Form-error detection | Raw-pixel self-supervised learning on real video | The method FitNova adopts |
| Gallardo et al., GYMetricPose [26] | Form-error detection | 2D to 3D pose graph with angular edges | Returns to a pose representation; reported second-hand with an unverified split |
| Dibenedetto et al., LMM [27] | Form-error detection | Fine-tuned LLaVA-Video, one model across exercises | Underperforms specialists, weak on rare errors, trains on validation, heavy |
| Papastratis, Kalpakoglou [15], [28] | Nutrition | Generative and language-model meal plans under constraints | Constraint drift risk; correctness not guaranteed |
| Majumder et al. [4] | Recipe generation | Personalized recipe text from user history | The text task FitNova reproduces; personalization not adopted |
