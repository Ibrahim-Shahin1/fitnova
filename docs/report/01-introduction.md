# 1 Introduction

FitNova is an AI fitness platform that brings workout planning, exercise form correction, and nutrition guidance into a single platform. This chapter explains what the project is and why it was built. It gives the background and the context of the work, states the problem that FitNova tries to solve, defines the scope and the objectives, and then describes the methodology and the work plan that were followed to build and evaluate the system.

## 1.1 Overview

Fitness applications that use artificial intelligence have become common. Instead of giving the same fixed program to everyone, they take a user profile (age, gender, body measurements, training goal, weekly availability) and try to produce a plan that fits that person [1]. This helps with planning, but two other parts of training are often left out. The first is technique. A workout plan tells a user what to do but not whether they are doing it safely, and a beginner who performs a movement with the wrong form can lose progress or get injured. The second is nutrition, which decides a large part of any fitness result and is often disconnected from the training goal. FitNova was built to cover all three of these areas in one place: planning, form correction, and nutrition.

The workout planning module takes the user profile and produces a seven-day plan. It first scores a catalogue of training programs against the profile, then re-ranks the best of them with a neural collaborative filtering model [2], and finally uses a small crew of language model agents to write the final plan using only real exercises from the dataset, so the plan can not contain invented movements. The form correction module looks at a video of the user performing an exercise and reports specific errors, for example the knees caving inward in a squat or the elbows drifting during an overhead press. Unlike many earlier systems that rely on pose estimation and fixed joint-angle rules, this module learns the errors directly from the pixels of the video, using deep networks trained on the Fitness-AQA dataset, which provides real gym footage annotated by experts [3]. The nutrition module turns the same profile into calorie and macronutrient targets, then builds a daily meal plan from a large public recipe dataset (Food.com) [4], with the numbers computed by fixed formulas so they are always correct.

One idea runs through the whole project. Each module is built on a dataset and a method that were already published, and each one is measured with the same metrics that the original work used. This way the results can be compared directly with prior published work, using the same measures those papers used. It also means the report can be honest about the parts that did not work, because the form correction module in particular went through several earlier designs that were dropped before the final one was reached.

## 1.2 Problem Statement

Many gym users still train without a clear, personalized plan. They either follow a generic routine that was written for a general audience, or they go to the gym with no concrete plan at all. A generic plan ignores the things that make each person different, like their experience level, their goal, and how much time they can train each week, so it is rarely the best plan for that user. Machine learning recommenders can fix part of this by learning a plan from the user profile [1], but most of them stop at choosing exercises or building a schedule. They do not tell the user whether the movements are being performed correctly, and they do not connect the training to what the user should eat.

Knowing what to do is only half of training. Doing it with correct form is the other half, and it is where beginners struggle the most. A squat that is too shallow, knees that collapse inward, or elbows that drift on an overhead press are small mistakes that lower the benefit of the exercise and, over time, can cause injury. Detecting these mistakes automatically is harder than it looks. The common approach is to estimate the body as a skeleton of joints and then apply fixed rules on the joint angles. This works in a clean, controlled setup, but it becomes unreliable on real gym video, where the camera angle, the clothing, the lighting, and parts of the body being hidden all interfere with the pose estimate [3]. A system that is meant to be trusted has to handle these real conditions, and it has to be checked against a real benchmark instead of against easy examples that were picked to make it look good.

Nutrition is the third gap. The result of any training program depends a lot on diet, yet many users have no structured plan that matches their calorie and macronutrient needs. AI tools can write meal plans, and generative recipe models can even produce full cooking instructions [4], but a generated plan can be wrong in two different ways. The numbers can be wrong, for example a plan that does not actually reach the calorie or protein target, or that suggests an unsafe intake. The text can also be wrong, for example a recipe that is invented or that does not match the meal it claims to be. A useful nutrition feature has to keep the numbers correct first, and only then make the plan readable and specific.

These three problems are usually handled by separate tools, and many of those tools are hard to trust because they are tested on data that does not represent real use. FitNova treats the three as one problem: build a single platform that plans training, checks form, and guides nutrition, where every part is built on real published data and measured the same way the original research measured it, so the result can be defended against published work rather than only demonstrated.

## 1.3 Scope and Objectives

FitNova is made for everyday gym users, mostly beginners and intermediate trainees, who want their planning, their form feedback, and their nutrition in one place instead of spread across several apps that do not talk to each other. The user gives a profile once, and the same profile drives all three modules. The platform does not try to be a medical product. It supports a healthy training lifestyle and gives decision support, and it does not give clinical diagnosis or replace the advice of a doctor or a qualified coach [5].

The three modules are each kept to what their data can actually support. The workout planner produces a seven-day plan from a catalogue of real training programs. The form correction module covers a fixed set of exercises and errors that come from the chosen dataset, the squat and the overhead press from video and a squat depth check from a single image, and it is judged on that dataset's in-the-wild test set. The module is not claimed to work on any casual front-facing phone clip, because that is a harder setting than the data was collected for, and the report is clear about this limit later. The nutrition module produces calorie and macronutrient targets and a daily meal plan from a public recipe set, as guidance rather than a prescription.

FitNova was built with the following objectives.

O1: Design an architecture that links personalized fitness planning, form correction, and nutrition guidance in a single AI platform, driven by one shared user profile.

O2: Build a fitness planning module that takes the user profile (age, body metrics, training goal, experience level, and weekly availability) and produces a personalized workout plan, using a recommendation pipeline that scores candidate programs and re-ranks them with a neural collaborative filtering model [2].

O3: Tailor the plan to the user's goal, so that different goals such as weight loss, muscle gain, or endurance lead to clearly different training, and ground the final plan in real exercises from the dataset so it can not contain invented movements.

O4: Build a form correction module that detects specific, named errors by learning directly from the raw video and images of a published in-the-wild dataset (Fitness-AQA) [3], instead of relying on pose estimation and fixed angle rules, and evaluate it with the same per-error F1 score that the original work reports.

O5: Build a nutrition module that computes calorie and macronutrient targets with a deterministic engine, selects real recipes that meet those targets and the user's constraints, and guarantees the nutritional correctness of the plan before any language model is used to describe it. This module also reproduces a published recipe-text generation method [4] as part of the nutrition work.

O6: Integrate the three modules into one working platform with a shared backend, so the output of one part can be used by the others.

O7: Build every module on a published dataset and method, evaluate it with the same metrics as that prior work for a fair comparison, and report the results honestly, including the earlier form correction designs that were tried and dropped.

## 1.5 Work Methodology

The methodology of this project rests on one main decision. Instead of designing new ad hoc methods and judging them only by how good the output looks, each module is built on a public dataset and a method that have already been published and reviewed, and each module is measured with the same metric that the original work used. This turns every part of FitNova into a reproduction that can be placed next to prior results and compared directly, and it gives an outside reader a fixed reference point to judge the system against. Each module's method is validated against that published baseline on real data, so the design rests on measured evidence rather than on appearances.

Because the quality of any learned model depends on the data it is trained on, the data is treated as the starting point of the methodology rather than an afterthought. For every dataset, the class balance, the meaning of each label, and the distribution of the inputs are studied before a model is designed, and the model and its training are then shaped around what the data actually contains. This matters most for the form-correction data, where the errors that the system has to detect are only a small fraction of the examples. Because of that imbalance the training uses a class-weighted loss, and the decision threshold for each error is chosen deliberately instead of left at one half, so that a rare but important error is not missed simply because it is rare.

For the workout planner the methodology is a layered recommendation pipeline rather than a single model. A content-based stage scores candidate programs against the user profile with interpretable weighted rules, a learned collaborative-filtering model then re-ranks the strongest candidates [2], and a language-model stage assembles the final seven-day plan. The layering is deliberate, because each stage covers a weakness of the others. The content-based rules work even for a brand new user who has no history for a learned model to use, the learned model picks up patterns that simple rules miss, and the language model turns the chosen program into a plan a person can actually read and follow. A hard constraint sits under the language stage so that it can only use real exercises from the dataset and can not invent movements, which keeps the readable output honest.

For form correction the methodology is to learn the errors directly from the raw pixels of the video and the images, with deep convolutional networks, instead of first reducing the body to a skeleton of joints and writing fixed rules on the angles [3]. Pose-based rules were set aside because a pose estimator is unreliable on real gym footage, where the camera angle, the clothing, and hidden body parts distort the skeleton, and a rule built on a wrong skeleton is wrong as well. The labelled error examples are few, so the methodology leans on self-supervised pretraining to make up for it. The network first learns a representation from a large pool of unlabelled clips through a task that needs no human labels, and it is fine-tuned on the small labelled set afterwards. A plain supervised model with no pretraining serves as the baseline, so the contribution of the pretraining is reported as a measured gain over it rather than assumed.

For nutrition the methodology keeps correctness and language apart. Every number, the calorie target, the macronutrient split, and the selection of recipes that fit them, comes from fixed formulas and explicit rules, so it is correct by construction. The language model is only allowed to describe and explain the plan that the deterministic part has already fixed. It can not change a number or pick a food. This makes a wrong calorie total or an invented recipe impossible by design, instead of an error that has to be found after the fact. The separate recipe-text generator is handled as a clean reproduction of a published model [4], trained and tested on the same task and the same split as the original, so its quality can be compared metric for metric.

The same thinking carries into evaluation. Each dataset is used with its official train, validation, and test split exactly as released, so every number in this report is computed on the same test data as the work it is compared against. Any threshold or decision cut is set on the validation split alone, and the test split is held back until the final measurement, so the reported scores are not quietly tuned to the test set. The metrics are taken from the source papers too, the F1 score for each form error [3], the hit rate and the normalised discounted cumulative gain for the recommender [2], and the standard text-overlap and diversity scores for the recipe generator [4], so that the comparison is like for like. The training and the test behaviour of each model are then compared, to confirm that a score comes from real generalisation and not from a model that has only memorised its training examples.

## 1.6 Work Plan (Gantt Chart)

The project followed a 30-week plan that ran from October to May, shown in the Gantt chart in Figure 1. The plan used an incremental, module-by-module approach [6], where the three modules were designed and built in their own overlapping windows rather than all at once, so that work on a later module could begin before the previous one was fully finished.

The first 4 weeks covered project initiation, the idea and the proposal. Related work and data gathering came next, from week 4 to week 11, running alongside the early planning, and this is where the datasets for the three modules were located and collected. Related work and data gathering were given a full eight weeks of their own, before any module was built, which matches the data-first approach set out in the methodology. The interim report was finalised and submitted around weeks 10 and 11.

Implementation then moved one module at a time. The fitness planning module was designed and built across weeks 12 to 17. The form correction module followed, from week 14 to week 20, overlapping the planning work. The nutrition guidance module ran from week 16 to week 22. The overlap between the three was planned on purpose, so the data preparation and the model training for one module could run while another was being written.

System integration and testing, which covers objective O6, took weeks 21 to 25, once the three modules existed and could be connected through the shared backend. The final report was written and reviewed across weeks 24 to 29, again overlapping the end of integration, and the final submission was made in week 30. The work kept to this schedule, and each phase was completed inside its planned window.
