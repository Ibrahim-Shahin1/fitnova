# 6 Results and Discussion

This chapter brings the numbers together and discusses what they mean. It takes each module in turn, the form models against the published method, the recommender, and the nutrition work, and then steps back to discuss the limits of the results, where they are strong and where they stop. The numbers here are the ones defined and tested in Chapter 5, drawn from the committed result files.

## 6.1 Form correction results

The form models were built to be compared, error for error, with the published Fitness-AQA method on the same official test split, and Table 8 makes that comparison. For each of the five errors it shows the project's plain supervised baseline, the deployed self-supervised model, and the two numbers the paper reports, its own self-supervised model and, where it gives one, its supervised baseline.

**Table 8. Form-error detection, FitNova against Parmar et al., on the official test split (F1 per error; FitNova's deployed score in bold where it beats the paper's self-supervised result).**

| Error | FitNova baseline | FitNova deployed (SSL) | Parmar SSL (paper) | Parmar baseline (paper) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Squat knees-inward (KIE) | 0.2857 | **0.4198** | 0.4186 | 0.2970 |
| Squat knees-forward (KFE) | 0.8000 | **0.8410** | 0.8338 | 0.8184 |
| OHP elbows | 0.4167 | 0.4474 | 0.4552 | — |
| OHP knees | 0.8069 | **0.8770** | 0.8452 | — |
| Shallow-squat depth | 0.8750 | **0.8902** | 0.8694 | — |

The headline is that the deployed models match or beat the published self-supervised result on four of the five errors and trail it only narrowly on the fifth. On the squat they reach 0.4198 on the rare inward-knee error and 0.8410 on the forward-knee error, both a touch above the paper's 0.4186 and 0.8338. On the overhead press they reach 0.8770 on the knee error, clearly above the paper's 0.8452, and 0.4474 on the elbow error, a little below the paper's 0.4552. On the squat depth image the model reaches 0.8902 against the paper's 0.8694. Figure 20 draws these side by side, and it shows two things at a glance: how close the project's bars sit to the paper's, and, on the squat, how far both self-supervised results stand above the plain supervised baseline.

**Figure 20. All five errors, FitNova against Parmar et al.** *[Insert docs/figures/all_errors_vs_paper.png]*

Read per exercise rather than per error, the result is a clean win on all three. Figure 21 shows the macro F1, the average of an exercise's errors that weights the rare and the common one equally: 0.6304 against the paper's 0.6262 on the squat, 0.6622 against 0.6502 on the overhead press, and 0.8902 against 0.8694 on the depth image. The depth result also sits above the other self-supervised methods the paper benchmarks for that error, which score around 0.83, so on that error the project's model is the strongest of the set.

**Figure 21. Per-exercise macro F1, FitNova against Parmar et al.** *[Insert docs/figures/cross_exercise_macro.png]*

Numbers this close to a published result invite the obvious worry, that they come from a model that has memorised its small training set rather than learned the errors, and Chapter 5 described the audit built to test exactly that. Its conclusion, drawn in Figures 22 and 23, is that the deployed models are not overfitting in the way that matters for deployment. Figure 22 puts the training, validation, and test score side by side for each deployed error, and the point to read off it is that validation tracks test for every one of them, the two never more than a few points apart. A model whose validation score predicts its test score is a model that will behave on new data as it did in development, which is what deployment needs.

**Figure 22. Deployed models: train, validation, and test F1 per error.** *[Insert docs/figures/summary_deployed_train_val_test.png]*

The same figure shows the honest other half of the picture. For the two rarest errors, the inward-knee squat and the press elbow, the training score sits far above the test score, about 0.93 against 0.42 for the inward-knee error. That gap is the mark of a model leaning on memorisation, which is hard to avoid when an error appears in only a hundred-odd training clips, and it caps how high those two errors can score. It does not break the result, because validation still tracks test and the test score still matches the published work, but it is a real limit and it is named as one.

Figure 23 shows how the self-supervised pretraining bears on this. For the overhead-press elbow error, the pretraining cuts the gap between training and test from about 0.53 down to 0.13, and the gap between validation and test from about 0.10 to 0.03, turning a badly overfitting supervised baseline into a deployed model that generalises. For the inward-knee squat error the same pretraining does something more honest to report: it lifts the test score from 0.29 to 0.42, a real gain, but it does so by fitting the training set harder, so the gap between training and test actually grows rather than shrinks. The lift is genuine and the test score matches the paper, yet the model reaches it partly by memorising more, and the figure shows that plainly rather than hiding it.

**Figure 23. The effect of self-supervised pretraining on the overfitting gaps.** *[Insert docs/figures/summary_ssl_fix.png]*

One last point sits in the same data and counts in the project's favour. The one model that does overfit in the deployment sense, with its validation score standing noticeably above its test score, is the overhead-press supervised baseline, the model that was measured but not deployed. That the discarded control overfits while the deployed models do not is itself evidence that the clean results are real rather than a lucky split.

## 6.2 Recommender results

The recommender was evaluated by the leave-one-out protocol of Section 5.2, over the 973 users, and the headline numbers are a hit rate at 10 of 0.9270 and a normalised discounted cumulative gain at 10 of 0.7469. Figure 24 places these next to the two halves of the model trained on their own, the generalised matrix factorisation part and the multi-layer perceptron part. The fused model is ahead of both on both measures: the matrix factorisation part reaches 0.9075 and 0.7251, the perceptron part 0.9065 and 0.7208, and joining them lifts the result to 0.9270 and 0.7469. The gain from the fusion is small on the hit rate and clearer on the ranking measure, which is the harder of the two, and this is the pattern the original method reports, that the two views of the interaction add something to each other [2].

**Figure 24. Recommender ranking quality, the fused model against its two halves.** *[Insert docs/figures/recommender/recommender_hr_ndcg.png]*

The caveat from Section 4.1 has to be carried into how this number is read. The model was trained and tested on synthetic interactions generated from a rule, so a hit rate of 0.9270 says that the neural model reconstructs that synthetic pattern very well. It is a faithful reproduction of the method on the data it was given, and it is not a measurement of how well FitNova would recommend to real users, which would need real interaction data to claim. The honest reading is that the modelling is sound and the evaluation is clean, while the data underneath is the part a deployed version would have to replace.

## 6.3 Nutrition results

The nutrition module produces two kinds of result, the quality of its recipe text and the correctness of its meal plans, and they are reported in turn.

For the recipe-text generator, Figure 25 draws the comparison from Table 7 against the paper's two learned models. On the overlap measures, the BLEU scores and ROUGE, and on the diversity measures, the two Distinct scores, the fine-tuned DistilGPT-2 sits above both of the paper's learned models on every bar. The clearest gaps are on the harder measures: the four-word BLEU score, where a small absolute number still represents close to double the paper's, and the two diversity scores, where the generated text is markedly more varied than the paper's models, which tend to repeat safe phrasings. The one place the model does not lead, as Table 7 records, is against the paper's non-learned nearest-neighbour baseline on the second diversity score, which is unsurprising, because that baseline simply returns a real recipe's text and real text is naturally varied. The framing from Chapter 4 holds here: this is a win on the quality of the generated text, measured the same way the paper measures it, and not a reproduction of the paper's personalisation, which conditioned on a user's history and which this work does not attempt.

**Figure 25. Recipe generation, DistilGPT-2 against the learned models of Majumder et al.** *[Insert docs/figures/nutrition/comparison.png]*

For the meal planner, the result is not a learned score but a guarantee, and the evidence for it is in Figures 26 and 27. Figure 26 plans a week for two contrasting profiles, a powerbuilding male on a gaining target of about 3,133 calories and a cutting female on a losing target of about 1,507, and shows the planned calories for each of the seven days against the target and a ten-percent band. Every day, for both profiles, lands on the target line, the average match being 99.8 percent for the first profile and 99.9 percent for the second. Figure 27 takes one of those days and shows the macronutrients the plan achieved against the targets it was given, 159 grams of protein against a target of 160, 448 grams of carbohydrate against 461, and 74 grams of fat against 72. These numbers are not learned and they do not vary from one run to the next; they are what the deterministic engine and selector produce by construction, and the figures are simply the proof that the construction holds across different bodies and goals.

Together the two halves of the nutrition module show the split the design was built around: a learned part that competes on the quality of its language, and a deterministic part whose numbers are correct by construction rather than by a model getting them right.

## 6.4 Discussion

The results across the three modules share a shape. Each one was built on a published dataset and method and measured the same way, and each one matched or beat the work it was set against: the form models on per-error F1 against Parmar et al. [3], the recipe generator on text-quality metrics against Majumder et al. [4], and the recommender as a faithful reproduction of neural collaborative filtering [2]. That is the strong part, and it is the part that makes the work defensible, because a number measured the same way as published work and reproducible from a committed file is a number an examiner can check. But every one of these results has a limit, and the limits are worth stating together in one place, because reading them honestly is the difference between a result and an overclaim.

The first limit is the two hardest errors. The inward-knee squat error and the elbow press error are rare, present in roughly one clip in seven and one in four, and the results showed both deployed models leaning partly on memorisation of their few hundred positive examples, with the training score far above the test score. This is a ceiling set by the data, not a flaw in the method. There are only so many examples of a rare error to learn from, and a small model trained on a small positive set will fit them closely; the audit's value is that it makes this visible rather than letting the high training score pass as success. The limit is bounded, because the validation score still predicts the test score and the test score still matches the published work, so the rare errors are as good as the published method manages and no worse. But they are the errors a user should trust least, and the honest position is that these two detectors sit at the edge of what the data supports.

The second limit is the largest, and it is the gap between the benchmark and the real world. Every form number in this report is measured on the Fitness-AQA test set, which is real gym footage but of a particular kind, shot from the side with the whole body in frame, the way the dataset was collected. On a casual clip filmed front-on at arm's length with a phone, the same models are close to random, because that input is outside the distribution they learned. A fix would not remove this, because it is the boundary of what a model trained on one kind of video can do, and it is the reason the user-facing form feature was reframed from a general camera tool into a benchmark inspector that runs on the dataset's own clips and says plainly where it is reliable. It would be easy to demonstrate the model on a phone and let the gap go unmentioned; the project chose instead to ship the honest version and to state the limit, here and in the interface. Closing this gap would need training data from the casual-phone domain, which the dataset does not provide, and that is left to the future work.

The third limit belongs to the recommender. Its hit rate of 0.9270 is a real number, computed cleanly by a standard protocol, but it is computed on interaction data that the project generated from a rule, because no real record of user choices exists. The number therefore measures how well the neural model reconstructs that synthetic pattern, and it is strong evidence that the modelling is implemented correctly, but it is not evidence about how the recommender would perform for real people, whose behaviour a rule only approximates. The content-based and goal-based parts of the pipeline work from the real user profile and do not depend on this data, so a user still gets a sensible, goal-matched plan, but the learned re-ranking layer is the part whose real-world value can not be claimed until real interaction data exists to retrain and re-measure it.

The fourth limit is narrower and was stated when the result was. The recipe generator beats the published models on the quality of its text, but the published work's actual contribution was personalisation, writing a recipe shaped by a particular user's history, and this reproduction does not do that. The comparison is fair on the ground it claims, text quality measured identically, and the project is careful not to let a win on quality be read as a win on the thing the paper set out to do. The deterministic side of the nutrition module carries no such caveat, because its guarantee is structural and holds for any profile, but the generative side competes only on language.

None of these limits undoes the central result, which is that each module does what published work does, measured the same way, and the form module does it while an independent audit confirms the deployed models generalise. What the limits mark out is the distance between a defensible benchmark result and a finished product. The form models are ready as a benchmark and not as a phone app; the recommender is a correct model waiting for real data; the nutrition module is a correct planner with a competitive but non-personalised text layer. Naming that distance plainly is itself part of the contribution, because the failure that this whole project was rebuilt to escape came from the opposite habit, of building on numbers that looked good and did not hold. The work that would carry each module past its current limit is the subject of the next chapter.
